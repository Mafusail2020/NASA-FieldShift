# Why rotate
A rotation breaks disease and pest cycles, spreads work and water use across the season, balances nitrogen and humus, and spreads weather risk. Growing the same crop repeatedly on one field raises disease pressure, so each crop has a minimum return interval.

# KMU 164 return intervals in the app
The app checks each rotation against minimum return intervals taken from KMU Resolution No. 164: winter wheat 2 years, other cereals and buckwheat 1 year (can follow after one year), and longer for sunflower, sugar beet and legumes in the full table. Maize is not given a return interval: the resolution allows 2-3 years in a row. A rotation that returns a crop sooner than its interval is flagged, not hidden.

# Predecessor rules of thumb
Winter wheat after pea, soybean, rapeseed or fallow is better than after wheat or maize. Legumes leave nitrogen. Deep-rooting, late-harvested crops (sunflower, sugar beet, maize) leave dry soil, which matters in dry regions. After maize, wheat needs residue management and often extra nitrogen.

# Water and heat risk in the app
The app runs a daily FAO-56 water balance for each rotation step on 1993-2025 NASA POWER weather. Water stress days are days when the crop's available soil water fell below the stress threshold during the crop's own season. Heat days count days over the crop's heat threshold in its sensitive window. Bare-soil months count months with no crop cover. Ks mid is the average water-stress factor in mid season, 1 means no stress, lower is worse.

# Soil effects
High salinity (ECe above a crop's threshold) lowers yield by a crop-specific percent per dS/m. The app treats a crop as ruled out when the remaining share falls below 50 percent. pH only warns, because published ECOCROP limits are probably too narrow for alkaline southern soils. Humus balance depends on crop residues, manure and green manure; black fallow mineralises humus.
